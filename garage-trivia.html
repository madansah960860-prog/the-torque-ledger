Garage Trivia

By the The Torque Ledger Editorial Team · Last reviewed: October 2026

Separate the old-car myths from the facts, pick up some workshop curiosities, and test your shop knowledge — with the answers tucked just out of sight until you are ready.

Myth-busting from the workshop

Old-car lore is full of half-truths passed down through the generations. Here are some common beliefs examined. Read each, decide what you think, then open it for the plain answer.

Myth: You should warm up a classic engine for several minutes before driving.

Mostly outdated. A brief warm-up to get the oil flowing is sensible, especially in the cold, but long idling wastes fuel and does little good. Gentle driving until the engine reaches temperature is kinder than prolonged idling for most cars. Always follow your manual.

Myth: Premium fuel is always better for a classic.

Not necessarily. Use the octane your engine was designed for, which is in the manual. Higher octane than the engine needs brings no benefit; too low can cause knocking in a high-compression classic. Match the fuel to the engine.

Myth: You never need to change oil if the car barely gets driven.

False. Oil degrades over time as well as with use, absorbing moisture and acids even while sitting. A little-driven classic still benefits from an annual oil change.

Myth: A car that sits is better preserved than one that is driven.

Generally false. Cars do better for gentle, regular use. Sitting leads to flat-spotted tires, stale fuel, seized parts, dried seals, and a flat battery. A classic driven now and then stays far healthier, so a gentle monthly outing is one of the best things you can do for a stored car.

Myth: Rust is only a cosmetic problem.

False, and dangerously so. Surface rust may be cosmetic, but structural rust in the frame or floors weakens the car and can make it unsafe. Rust must always be taken seriously.

Myth: Any mechanic can service a classic as easily as a modern car.

Not always. Classics use older systems — carburetors, points ignition, drum brakes — that many modern technicians rarely see. A specialist or an experienced classic mechanic is often worth seeking out.

Fun facts from the shop floor

A few workshop curiosities to enjoy and share.

  • The word "torque," which gives this Ledger its name, describes a twisting or turning force — exactly what an engine produces and what you apply with a wrench.
  • "Dwell," a term from points ignition, refers to how long the points stay closed during each cycle — an old measurement that puzzles many modern mechanics.
  • Grease fittings, common on classics and rare on modern cars, are why old-car owners talk about a "lube job" at every oil change.
  • Many classics used a manual choke the driver operated by hand to start a cold engine — a skill now almost forgotten.
  • The humble cotter pin and lock washer, found all over a classic, are reminders of an age when fasteners were expected to be checked and maintained.

A few more myths examined

The workshop is never short of old wives' tales. Here are more to weigh.

Myth: Thicker oil is always better for an old engine.

Not necessarily. Use the oil grade your engine was designed for, or a sensible modern equivalent for its age and condition. Oil that is too thick can struggle to circulate on a cold start, which is when most wear happens. Consult the manual or a specialist.

Myth: A classic should never be driven in the rain.

An overstatement. Many classics were daily drivers in all weather. Driving in the rain occasionally does no harm if the car is dried and cared for afterward; the real enemy is leaving it wet and dirty, and road salt in winter.

Myth: Modern fuel will destroy a classic engine.

Overblown, but with a grain of truth. Modern fuels differ from those of decades past, and ethanol content can affect old rubber and fiber parts over time. Many owners use fuel stabilizers and replace vulnerable parts with modern-resistant ones; a specialist can advise for your car.

Myth: If it runs, the brakes must be fine.

Dangerously false. An engine and brakes are entirely separate systems. A car can run beautifully and have brakes that are worn, leaking, or unsafe. Always have the brakes inspected, especially on a car that has been sitting.

Myth: More horsepower always means a better classic.

Not at all. Many of the most beloved classics are modest in power but rich in character, comfort, or beauty. The 'best' classic is the one that gives its owner the most pleasure, which has little to do with raw numbers.

Myth: Original paint is always better than a repaint.

It depends. Preservationists prize honest original paint, even worn, as unrepeatable history. But a quality repaint can protect and beautify a car with no surviving original finish. Neither is simply 'better' — it depends on the car and your goals.

Myth: You must store a classic with the tank empty.

Generally the opposite. A full tank with fuel stabilizer leaves less room for condensation to form inside, which helps prevent rust in the tank. Follow your manual and a trusted source for your car.

Test your shop knowledge

A few questions for the seasoned hand. Think it through, then reveal the answer.

What does a torque wrench measure, and why does it matter?

It measures the twisting force applied to a fastener, so you can tighten it to the exact specification. Correct torque prevents both loose parts and stripped or broken fasteners.

Why should you never open a hot radiator cap?

The cooling system is pressurized, and a hot system can erupt scalding coolant and steam when opened, causing serious burns. Always wait until the engine is completely cool.

What is the single most important safety rule when working under a car?

Never rely on a jack alone. Always support the raised car on proper jack stands on firm, level ground, and chock the wheels.

Why does old brake fluid need replacing even if the brakes work fine?

Brake fluid absorbs moisture from the air over time, which lowers its boiling point and can reduce braking performance. Periodic replacement keeps the system safe.

Why do little-driven classics still need regular oil changes?

Oil degrades with time as well as use, absorbing moisture and acids even while the car sits. An annual change protects a low-mileage classic just as much as a frequent one.

What simple habit prevents most roadside breakdowns in a classic?

Regular inspection and maintenance on schedule — checking fluids, belts, hoses, and tires, and fixing small issues before they grow. Most breakdowns are preventable with routine care.

Why keep a written log of everything you do to the car?

A logbook reminds you what has been done and what is due, helps a mechanic understand the car, and adds real value and interest for the next owner. A well-documented classic is a well-cared-for one.

What is the safest first step before working on any electrical part?

Disconnect the battery, removing the negative (ground) cable first. This prevents accidental shorts and sparks while you work on the wiring.

Why is it worth photographing a car before you take anything apart?

Because months later, those photographs will tell you exactly how a part fitted, which way a bracket faced, and where each wire ran — saving hours of puzzlement during reassembly.

More curiosities from the trade

A few more terms and oddments to enrich your workshop vocabulary.

  • A "tune-up," on an older car, traditionally meant servicing the ignition and fuel systems together — points, plugs, timing, and carburetor — to restore crisp running.
  • "TDC" stands for top dead center, the point where a piston is at the very top of its travel; it is the reference for setting ignition timing.
  • The "firing order" is the sequence in which an engine's cylinders fire — get the spark-plug wires in the wrong order and the engine will not run.
  • "Backfire" and "misfire" are different things: a misfire is a cylinder failing to fire properly, while a backfire is unburned fuel igniting in the exhaust or intake.
  • A "flathead" engine has its valves in the block beside the cylinders rather than overhead — a classic design that powered many beloved cars.

The quiet value of knowing

Why bother learning all this, in an age when a phone can summon help to the roadside? Because understanding your car changes your whole relationship with it. A knowledgeable owner is a confident owner: you can judge whether a noise is trivial or serious, whether a quoted repair is fair, and whether a job is within your reach or best left to a professional. That confidence is worth a great deal, and it is available to anyone willing to read and tinker a little.

There is also a deeper pleasure in it. To understand how your car works — to know what is happening beneath the hood as the engine idles — is to appreciate it the more, as one appreciates a piece of music better for knowing something of how it is made. The knowledge is its own reward, and it never stops growing. That, in the end, is why we keep a ledger at all: not merely to fix the car, but to understand and cherish it.

Keep the knowledge turning over

The pleasure of this hobby is that there is always more to learn, and a well-stocked mind is as useful in the garage as a well-stocked toolbox. Separating myth from fact, picking up the old terms, and understanding why things are done a certain way all make you a better, safer, and more confident owner.
